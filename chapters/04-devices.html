Programming, reading, and reliability

When does a larger memory window buy a better memory?

Write, wait, read, and repeat are different experiments

To evaluate a memory, first follow one cell through time. A write pulse is a voltage applied for a chosen duration to prepare a state. After that pulse, the cell waits under specified electrical and temperature conditions. A read applies another specified condition and measures a signal. Repeating this sequence can gradually change the device even if the early reads are successful.

Retention asks whether the written states remain distinguishable during the waiting interval. Endurance asks how repeated use changes the ability to write and read. Disturb means that an operation unintentionally changes a state, for example when a read or a neighboring write stresses a cell that should retain its value. These properties require different experiments; a single attractive hysteresis curve does not determine all three.

Different device structures make different signals accessible. A capacitor exposes transferred charge. A transistor translates an internal electrical state into a channel current. A tunnel junction places a sufficiently thin barrier between conductors that quantum-mechanical transmission contributes to current even when classical particles would not cross the barrier. The state can change that transmission. The following comparison explains what must be sensed in each case and what the read operation itself might alter.

Prerequisites: Chapters 1–3. Objectives: distinguish FeCAP, FeFET, and tunnel-junction sensing; relate programming to fields and time; separate memory window from retention and endurance; and derive a constrained reliability calculation with stated uncertainties.

4.1 Three ways to read polarization

A ferroelectric capacitor, or FeCAP, stores information in a polarization state between electrodes. A conventional switched-charge read applies a pulse and senses whether substantial switching charge appears; if the read switches the state, restoration is necessary. A field-effect transistor with a ferroelectric gate, or FeFET, uses polarization and associated charge to change channel conduction. Reading with a small gate bias can avoid intentional polarization reversal, but repeated reads can still disturb traps or state.

A ferroelectric tunnel junction uses a thin barrier whose transmission depends on the polarization state and interface potential. A low read voltage measures resistance rather than switched charge. The exponential sensitivity of tunneling to thickness can produce strong state contrast and strong variability simultaneously. None of these sensing methods is interchangeable with a conventional DRAM capacitor, whose stored free charge normally leaks away and requires refresh.

Let $V_{T,H}$ and $V_{T,L}$ be thresholds extracted at the same drain bias, current criterion, and delay after the two write operations. Define memory window $\Delta V_T=V_{T,H}-V_{T,L}$. The definition is operational. Swept-transfer hysteresis at one sweep rate is not necessarily the pulsed memory window relevant to use. A large polarization loop can coexist with a small read window because screening and semiconductor coupling intervene. Wang thesis, section 3.3.

4.2 Programming is a field-and-time problem

A write pulse must drive the internal ferroelectric field through a state-changing trajectory. Gate voltage alone does not specify that field because voltage also drops across the interface dielectric and semiconductor. Increasing pulse amplitude can switch more domains or switch them faster, while increasing electric stress and charge injection elsewhere. Pulse duration, rise time, polarity, and initial state must accompany a quoted programming voltage.

A useful empirical teaching law is $\tau(E)=\tau_0\exp(E_a/|E|)$, where $\tau$ is a characteristic switching time, $\tau_0$ a prefactor in seconds, and $E_a$ an activation-field parameter in V/m. This Merz-type form encodes rapid acceleration with field. It does not identify a unique nucleation mechanism or account for every film's distribution of switching times.

If switching is further idealized as a Poisson event with constant rate $1/\tau$ during a pulse of duration $t_p$, the probability of remaining unswitched is $p_f=\exp(-t_p/\tau)$. To require $p_f\le p_{\max}$, rearrange to $t_p\ge\tau\ln(1/p_{\max})$. This is a conditional deduction from an assumed hazard law, not an empirical reliability guarantee.

The project takes $\tau_0=1$ ns, $E_a=2$ MV/cm, and $t_p=100$ ns, all synthetic choices. It sweeps field from 0.5 to 3 MV/cm and finds the lowest sampled field meeting $p_f\le10^{-6}$. This constrained problem is more useful than minimizing voltage without a reliability requirement. Adding a measured distribution of switching times would change the required margin and should be the next experimental refinement.

4.3 Screening can help one observable and hurt another

A sheet of trapped charge partly screens polarization. Depending on location and sign, that can stabilize a polarized state, shift threshold, or reduce the electrically visible memory window. A larger threshold window in a multilayer structure can therefore reflect coupled polarization and charge dynamics rather than “more ferroelectricity.” The interlayer and oxidant studies are important precisely because they investigate such coupled mechanisms. Interfacial-charge study; ALD-oxidant study.

A minimal occupancy model makes the distinction concrete. Let $f_t\in[0,1]$ be the fraction of occupied traps. With capture time $\tau_c$ and emission time $\tau_e$, write $\dot f_t=(1-f_t)/\tau_c-f_t/\tau_e$. At fixed conditions, the equilibrium occupancy is $f_{t,\infty}=\tau_e/(\tau_c+\tau_e)$ and the relaxation time is $(1/\tau_c+1/\tau_e)^{-1}$. These expressions follow by collecting the constant and linear terms in the differential equation.

If trap density per area is $N_t$, the charge contribution is $Q_t=-qN_tf_t$ for electron traps under the stated convention. The threshold shift then includes $-Q_t/C_{\mathrm{eff}}$ with a location-dependent effective electrostatic coupling. Changing the delay after a pulse changes $f_t$ and hence the read threshold even if polarization stays fixed. That is why immediate read-after-write and long-delay read can tell different stories.

4.4 Retention requires a clock and a temperature

Retention is persistence of distinguishable information over a stated time under stated conditions. A static bistable energy function proves two minima, not a ten-year lifetime. Thermal fluctuations, depolarization, domain nucleation, charge emission, and interfaces provide escape pathways. The relevant activation barrier need not equal bulk barrier density multiplied by the whole device volume.

For a single thermally activated escape path, an illustrative mean time is $\tau_r=\tau_{r0}\exp(\Delta U/(k_BT))$, where $\Delta U$ is a barrier energy and $\tau_{r0}$ an attempt-time scale. Taking logarithms gives $\ln\tau_r=\ln\tau_{r0}+\Delta U/(k_BT)$. A straight line versus $1/T$ is consistent with one constant activation barrier; curvature or mechanism changes invalidate simple acceleration to room temperature.

Using an assumed 0.7 eV barrier and 1 ns prefactor gives a retention time of only hundreds of seconds at 300 K, not years. The point is to test the consequence of the assumption rather than choose a pleasing label. For equal barriers, changing from 300 K to 358 K accelerates the escape rate by $\exp[(\Delta U/k_B)(1/300-1/358)]$. Temperature uncertainty is amplified exponentially, so reporting a single lifetime without a model range is misleading.

The retention-surrogate preprint accelerates a TCAD-based retention calculation for a specified configuration. A surrogate trained against a simulator inherits the simulator's calibration limits. Fast agreement with TCAD does not independently validate ten-year measured retention, nor does inference speed include the one-time cost of generating its training data. Physics-informed retention analysis.

4.5 Endurance, first-switch damage, and test conventions

Endurance is the number of stress cycles before a defined failure criterion is crossed. The criterion could be memory-window closure, error probability, leakage, breakdown, or a read-current margin. Different criteria and pulse histories produce different reported cycle counts. Even “one cycle” must specify whether it includes both programming and erasing.

The first-switch study compares ferroelectric devices with an equivalent conventional MOS reference and uses electrical characterization to identify substantial interface damage from the first polarization-switching event. Later cycling generates additional traps. The physical lesson is that early damage need not be a tiny extrapolation of long-run fatigue. An aging model fitted only after conditioning can miss the initial transition. Ravikumar et al., first-switch study.

The radiation study adds another dimension: a device can preserve a window under particular ionizing doses while remaining limited by ordinary cycling or thermal processing. Radiation hardness is not synonymous with endurance or universal reliability. Dose rate, bias during exposure, particle or photon type, post-exposure annealing, and the comparison stack must accompany a ratio. The paper is evidence for a device-level comparison, not a space-qualified storage product. Laminated-stack radiation study.

4.6 Follow one bit through a complete operating cycle

Our running bit stores a binary value in a threshold distribution, not in a single exact threshold. A write command creates a distribution of internal states; a later read maps those states into an electrical signal; a decision circuit maps the signal into a digital value. Let $b\in\{0,1\}$ be the intended bit, $P$ polarization, $f_t$ trap occupancy, and $V_T(P,f_t)$ the threshold under specified read conditions. The full state includes at least $(P,f_t)$ and may also need a damage variable. Two devices with identical immediate threshold can evolve differently if their polarization and trapped-charge contributions differ.

Separate four intervals: preparation, programming, settling, and reading. Preparation fixes the starting state and cycling history. Programming applies a specified pulse. Settling permits fast electrical transients and slower charge redistribution to evolve. Reading samples the resulting state at a chosen bias. If a paper changes the settling delay between experiments, it changes the observable. Treating all threshold extractions as the same memory window can mistake relaxation for improved switching.

A nondestructive read means that the intended read does not deliberately reverse the stored polarization. It does not mean the read has zero effect on traps or defect migration. A repeated small perturbation can accumulate over many accesses. Let $p_d$ be a simplified independent disturb probability per read. After $N$ reads the probability of at least one disturb event is $1-(1-p_d)^N\simeq Np_d$ when $Np_d$ is small. A per-read effect below immediate measurement resolution may still matter at workload scale. Correlated or history-dependent disturb needs a more complete model.

The memory specification therefore needs a read-count dimension as well as a retention-time dimension. A cold stored model weight and a hot cache line can experience the same elapsed time with radically different electrical histories. A device technology suitable for one role need not satisfy the other. This is why the architectural discussion must begin with access patterns rather than a generic label such as nonvolatile memory.

4.7 Programming voltage is distributed across a nonlinear stack

Let a ferroelectric layer of thickness $t_f$ have background permittivity $\epsilon_f=\epsilon_0\epsilon_b$ and switchable polarization $P$. Put it in series with a linear dielectric of thickness $t_i$ and absolute permittivity $\epsilon_i^{\rm abs}$. Temporarily neglect semiconductor voltage, trapped sheets, and leakage. If displacement $D$ is continuous, the terminal voltage is $V=t_f(D-P)/\epsilon_f+t_iD/\epsilon_i^{\rm abs}$. Solving for $D$ gives

$$D=\frac{V+(t_f/\epsilon_f)P}{t_f/\epsilon_f+t_i/\epsilon_i^{\rm abs}},\qquad E_f=\frac{D-P}{\epsilon_f}.$$

At fixed polarization, the incremental voltage division is determined by the two thickness-to-permittivity ratios. During switching, polarization changes and therefore changes the voltage division itself. Assigning $E_f=V/t_f$ ignores both effects. Adding a semiconductor introduces its surface potential and charge response; adding a trapped sheet makes displacement discontinuous by the sheet charge. The simple series formula is useful because each omitted element has a visible place to enter.

For the 10 nm, relative-background-permittivity-33 teaching film and a 1 nm, relative-permittivity-3.9 interface, the linear thickness-to-permittivity ratios are about 0.303 and 0.256 nm respectively. If polarization were momentarily fixed at zero, only about 54.2% of a small terminal voltage would drop across the ferroelectric. The rest would stress the much thinner interface. This calculation explains why a seemingly modest terminal voltage can damage a barrier while still failing to deliver the assumed switching field. Once polarization changes, the field must be recomputed self-consistently.

Wang's thesis section 3.3 goes beyond a purely capacitive picture by including screening current in a specific p-channel ZrO₂ device. The reported 3.4 V and −7 V pulses are terminal conditions for that stack, with 10 µs duration and a −50 mV read drain bias. They are not universal programming voltages. The empirical leakage parameters and missing current prefactor are reasons the delivered course does not claim an author-calibrated cell model. A realistic write simulation needs charge continuity over time, including how injected charge alters the next instant's field.

4.8 Derive the speed–field frontier and expose its tail risk

For the assumed single-rate switching law, a failure target $p_*$ requires $t_p/[\tau_0\exp(E_a/E)]\ge\ln(1/p_*)$ for positive field magnitude $E$. Rearranging gives a lower bound on field:

$$E\ge\frac{E_a}{\ln\!\left[t_p/(\tau_0\ln(1/p_*))\right]},\qquad t_p\gt\tau_0\ln(1/p_*).$$

The second inequality is a feasibility condition. As field tends to infinity, the assumed characteristic time approaches $\tau_0$ rather than zero. No finite field can meet the target when the allowed pulse is shorter than $\tau_0\ln(1/p_*)$. Near that boundary the required field diverges. An optimizer that simply returns a huge field has found the edge of its mathematical model, not a usable engineering solution. Breakdown, voltage-driver limits, and model validity must be additional constraints.

With the stated synthetic $\tau_0=1$ ns, $E_a=2$ MV/cm, and $p_*=10^{-6}$, the continuous field thresholds are 5.4063, 1.5549, 1.0104, and 0.7484 MV/cm for pulse widths 20, 50, 100, and 200 ns. The original finite-grid project reports 1.025 MV/cm at 100 ns because it chooses the first feasible sampled value. The depth laboratory now computes the analytic frontier and substitutes each point back into the survival law. Agreement distinguishes grid resolution from an error in the physics algebra.

A real distribution of switching times can invalidate the single-rate error estimate severely. Suppose half the cells have characteristic time 2 ns and half 20 ns under the same pulse. For a 100 ns pulse, the mixture failure probability is $(e^{-50}+e^{-5})/2=0.003369$. Replacing those two rates by their average predicts about $1.14\times10^{-12}$. The slower population dominates failures even though the mean rate looks favorable. This is a nine-order-of-magnitude warning against extrapolating a central switching metric into a low-error guarantee.

The inequality behind the example is Jensen's inequality: because $e^{-t\lambda}$ is convex in the nonnegative rate $\lambda$, averaging survival probabilities gives at least the survival evaluated at the average rate. Heterogeneity therefore worsens the single-average-rate estimate under this mixture model. The amount depends on the distribution, particularly its slow tail. Measuring only a median switching time cannot characterize that tail. A memory array with billions of opportunities for error can be dominated by a small subpopulation invisible in a typical-device plot.

4.9 Retention extrapolation is an inference problem

For a single Arrhenius mechanism, define the rate $\lambda(T)=\lambda_0\exp[-U/(k_BT)]$, with activation energy $U$ and attempt rate $\lambda_0$. If a constant failure probability is the target, equivalent observation times scale inversely with rate. The acceleration factor from low temperature $T_l$ to high temperature $T_h$ is $A=\lambda(T_h)/\lambda(T_l)=\exp[(U/k_B)(1/T_l-1/T_h)]$. The sign is positive for $T_h\gt T_l$: the hotter experiment reaches the same modeled damage sooner.

The logarithmic sensitivity to activation energy is $\partial\ln A/\partial U=(1/k_B)(1/T_l-1/T_h)$. A modest energy uncertainty can produce a large multiplicative uncertainty in equivalent lifetime. Worse, if one mechanism dominates at high temperature and another at low temperature, there is no single activation energy to extrapolate. A straight line over a narrow temperature interval cannot exclude such a crossover outside the measured range. Separate confidence in the fitted slope from confidence in the mechanism remaining unchanged.

Retention is often assessed through a continuously drifting signal rather than an observed binary escape event. If window decays as $W(t)=W_0e^{-t/\tau}$ under a teaching model and the read system needs $W\ge W_{\min}$, usable retention is $t_{\rm use}=\tau\ln(W_0/W_{\min})$. Doubling initial window increases usable time by $\tau\ln2$, rather than multiplying lifetime by two. The required read margin is an architectural parameter, so two arrays using the same device can report different useful retention under different error targets.

The physics-informed retention surrogate addresses computational cost within a specified TCAD setup. To evaluate such a surrogate, distinguish interpolation over parameters used during training from prediction for a new stack or trap mechanism. Check both trajectory error and error in the time at which the signal crosses the read threshold. A small average trajectory error can cause a large lifetime error when the trajectory is nearly flat near the crossing. Faster simulation is valuable, but it does not remove the need for measured calibration or uncertainty propagation.

4.10 What can a finite reliability experiment establish?

Suppose $N$ independent, identically distributed write attempts produce no failures. Let $p$ be the unknown failure probability under exactly that protocol. The probability of this outcome is $(1-p)^N$. A one-sided 95% upper confidence bound solves $(1-p_{95})^N=0.05$, giving $p_{95}=1-0.05^{1/N}\simeq2.996/N$. Thus zero failures in one million genuinely independent trials supports an upper bound around $3\times10^{-6}$, not zero error and not automatically $10^{-9}$ reliability.

Repeated trials on the same aging device may violate identical-distribution and independence assumptions. A thousand devices each tested once probes a different source of variation from one device cycled a thousand times. Report the hierarchy: wafer, die, device, cycle, read, and environmental condition. A pooled failure fraction can hide a small group of systematically weak devices. Confidence intervals computed as if every read were independent can then be far too narrow.

Censoring matters as well. A device that remains functional when the experiment stops supplies a lower bound on its lifetime, not an exact lifetime. A device measured only after blocks of cycles has a failure interval rather than a known failure cycle. A Weibull model, if chosen, writes survival as $S(t)=\exp[-(t/\eta)^\beta]$, with scale $\eta\gt0$ and shape $\beta\gt0$. Its hazard is $h(t)=(\beta/\eta)(t/\eta)^{\beta-1}$. Increasing hazard for $\beta\gt1$ is consistent with aging, while a constant hazard occurs at $\beta=1$. Fitting such a model does not itself identify a microscopic failure mechanism.

The first-switch damage study is especially relevant to model choice because an abrupt early change can coexist with slower later degradation. A single smooth cycle-count law fitted after conditioning can miss the preparation cost. The radiation study adds another stress axis with its own protocol. Neither result should be folded into one unqualified reliability number. A useful evidence table specifies stress, observable, sample hierarchy, failure criterion, and whether failures or only signal shifts were observed.

1. Why does a larger immediate window not guarantee longer retention?

Device A starts at 0.6 V and decays with time constant 100 s. Device B starts at 0.4 V and decays with time constant 300 s. The read circuit requires 0.2 V. Compare their usable times under exponential decay.

Solution. A remains above threshold for $100\ln3=109.86$ s. B lasts $300\ln2=207.94$ s. The smaller initial window lasts almost twice as long because its relaxation is slower. The threshold criterion and decay model are part of the answer. If trapping produces a nonmonotonic trajectory, even this crossing calculation must be revised; immediate read margin is not a substitute for time-resolved evidence.

2. Design a write experiment for the slow tail

The mean-rate calculation predicts an error far below the mixture result. What data would distinguish them?

Solution. Measure switching probability over a range of pulse widths at fixed internal-field conditions and controlled initial state, across many devices. A single exponential gives a straight line in log survival versus duration. A mixture generally curves as the fast population disappears and the slow population dominates. Preserve failed attempts rather than averaging only successful switching times. Repeat at additional fields to see whether a common rate distribution explains the change. Rare-event targets still need sufficient trial counts or a defensible tail model.

3. Establish a lower bound on evidence for a billion-to-one claim

Under independent identical trials, how many zero-failure attempts are needed for a one-sided 95% upper bound of $10^{-9}$?

Solution. Solve $1-0.05^{1/N}\le10^{-9}$, or $N\ge\ln(0.05)/\ln(1-10^{-9})$, approximately $3.0\times10^9$. Accelerated testing or a physical model can change how evidence is gathered, but introduces additional assumptions. Billions of reads of a single unchanged state do not automatically establish billions of independent write trials. The correct sample unit must match the claimed event.

4.11 Compare sensing mechanisms through their derivatives

The same polarization state can be read through switched charge, threshold modulation, or tunnel transmission. To compare those mechanisms, ask how a small physical variation changes the read signal. The derivative of the signal with respect to geometry or internal state is as important as its nominal magnitude. A highly sensitive mechanism can provide a large contrast and a large process-variation penalty simultaneously.

For an ideal rectangular tunnel barrier, an electron with energy below barrier height has a decaying wavefunction inside the barrier. Let $t_b$ be barrier thickness, $m_b$ an effective tunneling mass, and $\Phi$ the barrier energy measured above the electron energy in joules. The decay constant is $\kappa=\sqrt{2m_b\Phi}/\hbar$. In a thick-barrier approximation, transmission has dominant dependence $\mathcal T\propto e^{-2\kappa t_b}$. This is the leading exponential from solving the constant-barrier Schrödinger equation; matching at interfaces contributes prefactors.

Taking logarithms reveals the sensitivity: $\partial\ln\mathcal T/\partial t_b=-2\kappa$, and $\partial\ln\mathcal T/\partial\Phi=-\kappa t_b/\Phi$. A polarization-dependent barrier shift can therefore create a large resistance ratio. A small thickness variation can create a similarly large uncontrolled variation. The model omits image-force lowering, nonrectangular barriers, defects, and inelastic transport. Its purpose is to expose why a large tunnel electroresistance ratio is not sufficient to select a dense reliable memory.

For a synthetic $m_b=0.2m_e$, where $m_e$ is free-electron mass, and $\Phi=1$ eV, the decay constant is about 2.29 nm⁻¹. Increasing thickness by only 0.1 nm multiplies the leading transmission by $e^{-0.458}\simeq0.633$. This approximately 37% decrease follows before any change in polarization. A measured conductance histogram may therefore reflect geometric variation as strongly as stored-state variation. Absolute current, distributions, and breakdown constraints must accompany a ratio between two mean resistances.

For the FeFET linear-read model, the derivative with respect to threshold is $\partial I_D/\partial V_{\rm th}\simeq-\mu C_i(W/L)V_D$. Unlike the tunnel exponential, this local relation is linear under its assumptions. Yet mobility and contacts introduce separate multiplicative uncertainties. A capacitor's switched-charge read instead has ideal signal proportional to area times polarization change, while bit-line capacitance dilutes the voltage. No sensing method removes the need to propagate its own variation and loading into error probability.

Use pulse timing to separate state components

The trap occupancy law has an exact solution during an interval with fixed capture and emission rates: $f_t(t)=f_\infty+[f_t(0)-f_\infty]e^{-t/\tau_t}$, where $\tau_t=(\tau_c^{-1}+\tau_e^{-1})^{-1}$. After a write, the bias changes, so the rates and equilibrium occupancy can change. Apply the solution piecewise, carrying the final occupancy of one interval into the next. Resetting occupancy to its new equilibrium at every bias change would erase the transient the model is meant to explain.

Suppose, as a teaching construction, polarization contributes a stable 0.3 V window while trapped charge contributes another 0.2 V immediately after writing and decays with a 1 ms time constant. The apparent window is $W(t)=0.3+0.2e^{-t/(1\,\mathrm{ms})}$ V. It is 0.5 V immediately, about 0.374 V at 1 ms, and 0.301 V at 5 ms. Calling the immediate 0.5 V a stable polarization-only window would overstate the persistent contribution by two thirds. Conversely, a decaying trap contribution of the opposite sign could make the measured window grow after writing even with fixed polarization.

This example motivates matched delayed-read measurements in the screening-current analysis and the interface-damage study. A delay sweep can reveal multiple timescales, but fitting exponentials alone does not uniquely assign each timescale to a microscopic trap. Add bias and temperature dependence and independent characterization. The scientific task is to constrain a common state model across observations, rather than give a different unconstrained exponential to every curve.

4. Compare resistance ratio with absolute signal

A tunnel device improves its on/off ratio by reducing both currents, with off current falling faster. Must sensing become easier?

Solution. No. A ratio can increase while the absolute current difference shrinks. Charging the same bit-line capacitance to a required voltage then takes longer, and leakage or amplifier offset can become a larger fraction of the signal. Evaluate absolute current distributions at the stated read voltage, not only their ratio. The relevant objective is reliable sensing within the permitted latency and energy, which connects this device comparison to the next chapter.

4.12 Two models, two questions

Run python -X utf8 projects/stack.py switching. Its first calculation integrates a dimensionless double-well relaxation law, $\tau\dot x=x-x^3+v$, with $\tau=20$ ns. It tests whether a pulse moves the state from the negative well to the positive well and whether the state remains after the pulse. Its second calculation uses the separate empirical switching-time law above. The two models are deliberately not fitted to each other; they answer different teaching questions.

A complete device optimization would minimize write energy or voltage while constraining error, latency, retention, endurance, and breakdown. The present project solves only the stated switching constraint over a finite field grid. It is a useful baseline because its omissions are named and its limiting behavior can be checked.

4.13 Exercises and solutions

5. Pulse duration for a reliability target

If the characteristic switching time is 5 ns under a constant-hazard approximation, what pulse width makes unswitched probability at most $10^{-6}$?

Solution. $t_p\ge5\ln(10^6)$ ns, or approximately 69.08 ns. This excludes pulse rise time, distributions, and write verification. A measured long tail can require a larger duration than this single-rate estimate.

6. Trap relaxation after a write

Take $\tau_c=2$ µs and $\tau_e=8$ µs. Find equilibrium occupancy and relaxation time.

Solution. $f_{t,\infty}=8/(2+8)=0.8$. The effective rate is $1/2+1/8=0.625$ µs⁻¹, so the time constant is 1.6 µs. Starting empty, $f_t(t)=0.8[1-e^{-t/(1.6\,\mu s)}]$. The threshold contribution follows from the trap sheet and electrostatic coupling, not from occupancy alone.

7. An infeasible optimum

An unconstrained optimizer selects the minimum tested write voltage, but its switching error is 0.1. The system target is $10^{-6}$. Can the voltage be reported as optimal?

Solution. Only for the unconstrained mathematical problem, which is the wrong engineering problem. It is infeasible for the stated system. Add the reliability constraint and rerun the search; do not conceal the violation in a weighted average of energy and error.

8. Reliability evidence audit

A paper reports a million cycles but samples the memory window only every thousand cycles. What remains uncertain?

Solution. Failures between observations, intermittent read errors, the precise threshold-crossing cycle, and the reliability of individual accesses. The observed endurance is interval-censored relative to the sampling schedule. Reproduce the stress/read sequence and use a criterion matched to the intended use.

You can now state which device metric a measurement establishes and which reliability dimensions remain open. The next chapter asks whether that device can still be read and programmed inside an array.
