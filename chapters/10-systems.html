Servers, racks, and useful work

When does a more efficient device reduce facility energy?

A device can use one tenth as much switching energy while the server consumes almost the same power. It can also enable more useful work under a fixed power cap, leaving total energy unchanged. Both are reasonable outcomes. The question is which resource and denominator the comparison holds fixed.

Prerequisites: Chapters 5, 6, and 9; probability and calculus. Objectives: build a non-overlapping energy budget, derive a queueing limit, analyze communication and thermal constraints, distinguish fixed-work savings from capacity expansion, and translate a system target into a requirement on lower layers.

10.1 Account for energy once

Let $r$ denote useful output rate in tokens/s and $e_d$ the modeled dynamic energy per token in J/token. Let $P_0$ be baseline IT power in watts, covering the portion not already counted in $r e_d$. Then $P_{IT}=P_0+r e_d$ and $e_{IT}=P_{IT}/r=P_0/r+e_d$. This decomposition is a model: baseline power can depend on clock state, temperature, memory residency, and workload. A power-meter trace is needed to fit it.

Our cumulative scenario uses $P_0=45$ W and $e_d=84.3243$ µJ/token, with the latter containing modeled memory traffic and arithmetic once each. The 45 W is an explicit assumption, not an idle-power measurement of a named accelerator. It dominates this deliberately small teaching workload. That dominance makes a useful lesson visible: optimizing a tiny dynamic term can have a small effect on the total.

Facility power also includes power conversion, cooling, and other overhead. Power usage effectiveness, PUE, is defined as facility energy divided by IT-equipment energy over the specified accounting interval. In an illustrative steady scenario we use a constant multiplier $p$ and write $P_{facility}=pP_{IT}$. A reported annual PUE should not automatically be interpreted as an instantaneous multiplier during a short inference job. The boundary and averaging interval matter. DOE data-center design guide.

For steady operation, facility energy per useful output is $e_f=p(P_0/r+e_d)$. This is not a universal carbon metric. Carbon intensity depends on location and time, while water, embodied impacts, and hardware replacement require additional boundaries. For this course, the defensible endpoint is operational energy under stated assumptions.

10.2 Why maximum utilization is not the answer

A server with mean service time $s$ per batch has service rate $\mu=1/s$ batches/s. Let batches arrive at rate $\lambda$. Utilization is $\rho=\lambda/\mu=\lambda s$. For a single-server queue with Poisson arrivals and exponentially distributed independent service times, the stationary occupancy probabilities satisfy $\pi_{n+1}=\rho\pi_n$, because arrival flow from state $n$ balances service flow from state $n+1$. Normalization gives $\pi_n=(1-\rho)\rho^n$ for $0\le\rho\lt1$.

Summing $n\pi_n$ gives mean number in the system $L=\rho/(1-\rho)$. Little's relation, $L=\lambda W$, connects mean occupancy to mean arrival rate and mean response time. Therefore

$$W=\frac{s}{1-\rho},\qquad W_q=W-s=\frac{\rho s}{1-\rho}.$$

$W$ includes service; $W_q$ is waiting only. The divergence near full utilization follows from the assumptions, not from thermal physics. It shows why headroom is required under variable arrivals. Real inference serving has batching, variable prompt lengths, preemption, correlated arrivals, and different prefill/decode stages. An M/M/1 mean cannot certify a p99 latency target. Trace-driven simulation or measured service distributions are needed for that.

In the scenario, one 16-token batch step takes $s=0.6394304$ ms. A mean response target $W\le5$ ms implies $\rho\le1-s/0.005=0.87211392$. Our discrete sweep tries 0.1, 0.3, 0.5, 0.7, 0.9, and 0.98. Hence 0.7 is the largest feasible tested utilization. It is optimal only among those candidates; a refined search could approach the analytic boundary. This batch-response metric is neither measured end-to-end request latency nor an individual token's p99 delay.

10.3 Work the full budget

At utilization 0.7, modeled throughput is 17,515.589 tokens/s. Multiplying by 84.3243 µJ/token gives 1.476989 W dynamic power. Adding 45 W gives 46.476989 W IT power. At assumed PUE 1.2, facility power is 55.772387 W and facility energy is 3.184157 mJ/token. Dividing power by rate produces joules per token; multiplying them would have the wrong units.

The mean response is 2.131435 ms. At utilization 0.9, energy per token falls further to about 2.499 mJ/token, but response rises to 6.3943 ms and violates the constraint. An unconstrained energy optimizer would recommend an unacceptable operating point. The constrained problem is to minimize $e_f$ over admissible utilization, subject to response and IT-power limits, while keeping workload, numerical quality, and capacity unchanged.

Uncertainty matters more than the last decimal place. Holding the selected rate fixed, changing baseline power from 25 to 80 W and PUE from 1.1 to 1.5 yields a modeled range of about 1.663 to 6.978 mJ/token. These are scenario bounds, not statistical confidence intervals. A measured baseline-power curve would reduce the largest uncertainty in this example.

10.4 Fixed architecture versus redesign

Suppose a fraction $f$ of baseline execution time is spent in an operation accelerated by a factor $g$, while all other work remains unchanged. Normalize old time to one. New time is $(1-f)+f/g$, so speedup is $S=1/[(1-f)+f/g]$. This derivation requires an actual time fraction, not an energy fraction. If memory-device switching occupies 5% of time and becomes ten times faster, total speedup is only 1.04712.

For energy at fixed useful work, use a separately measured energy fraction $f_E$. New energy ratio is $(1-f_E)+f_E/g_E$ if the affected energy decreases by factor $g_E$. With $f_E=0.05$ and $g_E=10$, the ratio is 0.955: a 4.5% saving. A faster execution time could also change baseline energy, but that must be recomputed from the power-time model. Multiplying unrelated advertised speed and energy improvements usually double-counts or combines incompatible conditions.

A redesigned architecture could move more work into the improved resource. If the affected energy fraction were 70%, the same tenfold local reduction would give ratio 0.37, a 63% reduction under that different budget. The 70% is a hypothetical redesign assumption, not evidence supplied by a ferroelectric device paper. It requires implementation evidence for the new data path, converters, control, communication, accuracy, and utilization.

At a fixed facility power cap, efficiency may instead permit more servers or more throughput. If demand expands accordingly, total facility energy can remain constant or grow while energy per useful result falls. Report both quantities. More generated tokens are useful only if quality and task completion are comparable.

10.5 Communication can replace memory as the bottleneck

For a message of $M$ bytes over an effective link bandwidth $\mathcal B_n$, a simple transfer model is $t_n=\alpha+M/\mathcal B_n$, where $\alpha$ is fixed latency. Bandwidth improvements help large transfers; fixed latency dominates small messages. Effective bandwidth includes protocol, contention, topology, and collective-algorithm effects. It is not the port's advertised line rate.

For a ring all-reduce across $n$ participants with a payload of $M$ bytes per participant, an idealized reduce-scatter plus all-gather transfers $2(n-1)M/n$ bytes per participant over $2(n-1)$ stages. The corresponding estimate is $t_{AR}\approx2(n-1)\alpha+2(n-1)M/(n\mathcal B_n)$. This is a derivation for that schedule; other collectives and networks differ. At eight participants, 1 GB payload, 25 GB/s effective bandwidth, and 2 µs per stage, the estimate is 70.028 ms. Those are teaching inputs, not an installed cluster.

Training repeatedly computes gradients and updates weights. Gradients, optimizer state, activations, and communication can exceed the stored model-weight footprint. Nonvolatile storage can help checkpointing and data loading, but its value for high-frequency gradient updates depends on write latency, endurance, working-memory integration, and synchronization. Faster local memory leaves a non-overlapped 70 ms communication phase intact. Overlap can help, but requires a dependency-aware schedule and cannot exceed the available independent work.

10.6 Thermal and rack limits constrain throughput

A first steady thermal model is $T_j=T_a+R_\theta P$, where $T_j$ is junction temperature, $T_a$ an effective ambient temperature, and $R_\theta$ thermal resistance in K/W. With $T_a=30$ °C, limit $T_j=90$ °C, and $R_\theta=0.3$ K/W, the implied power ceiling is 200 W. This one-node approximation misses hotspots, transient thermal capacitance, neighboring dies, coolant variation, and temperature-dependent leakage. Package-level thermal modeling is required before using the value as a design limit.

A rack's electrical and cooling limits may bind separately. More memory density may increase usable capacity yet worsen local heat flux, yield, repair difficulty, or power delivery. A material surviving a fabrication anneal does not establish acceptable operating temperature or endurance in a stacked package. These distinctions connect back to Chapter 2's processing conditions and Chapter 8's integration budget.

To reason downward, start with required useful throughput, latency distribution, power budget, and capacity. Derive sustainable bandwidth and communication needs. Translate those into array concurrency, wire lengths, sensing margins, and write duty cycles. Only then request the device's window, noise, retention, endurance, and pulse statistics at the relevant temperature. This chain turns a broad system ambition into an experimentally answerable specification.

10.7 Derive a tail constraint instead of relabeling the mean

The running scenario selected utilization 0.7 under a 5 ms mean batch-response target. A user-facing service often cares about a percentile: for example, at least 99% of responses below 5 ms. Those are different specifications. Even within the same M/M/1 queue, the mean does not determine an acceptable tail without the distributional model. We can derive that distribution here rather than assigning the mean to every request.

Under stationary Poisson arrivals, an arriving job sees the same occupancy distribution as a random time. If it finds $n$ jobs in the system, it must wait through $n+1$ exponential service intervals, including its own. Memorylessness makes the unfinished service interval exponential with the same rate $\mu$. The conditional response density is therefore the Erlang density $\mu^{n+1}t^ne^{-\mu t}/n!$ for $t\ge0$. Weighting by $\pi_n=(1-\rho)\rho^n$ and summing gives

$$f_W(t)=(1-\rho)\mu e^{-\mu t}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\rho\mu t)^n}{n!}=(\mu-\lambda)e^{-(\mu-\lambda)t}.$$

The exponential-series identity is the hinge: mixing the conditional Erlang distributions yields an exponential total response distribution. Thus $\Pr(W\gt t)=e^{-(\mu-\lambda)t}$ and the 99th percentile is $W_{99}=\ln(100)/(\mu-\lambda)=\ln(100)s/(1-\rho)$. This is a derived result for the specified queue, not an empirical law for inference servers.

With $s=0.6394304$ ms and $\rho=0.7$, mean response is 2.13143 ms but the 99th percentile is about 9.816 ms. The scenario satisfies the 5 ms mean target and fails the 5 ms p99 target. Requiring $W_{99}\le5$ ms instead gives $\rho\le0.411063$. The depth laboratory checks this bound alongside the earlier mean limit 0.872114. Among the original utilization grid points, 0.3 would be the largest feasible choice for the p99 requirement under this model.

Real serving is not an M/M/1 queue merely because requests arrive online. Batches may be formed by a scheduler, service depends on context and batch composition, and prefill competes with decoding. The mathematical exercise is valuable because it exposes what the original mean-only calculation omitted. To apply a percentile requirement to a real system, preserve the arrival process, batching policy, service distribution, and response boundary in a trace-driven model or measurement.

10.8 Service variability changes waiting even at the same utilization

Consider a single-server, first-come-first-served queue with Poisson arrivals and independent service duration $S$ drawn from a general distribution. Let $s=\mathbb E[S]$, $\rho=\lambda s\lt1$, and assume finite second moment. An arrival waits for the unfinished service in progress plus all complete services already queued. Let $R$ be the residual service seen at arrival, with zero residual when the server is idle. Averaging the triangular residual-time area during each service interval gives $\mathbb E[R]=\lambda\mathbb E[S^2]/2$.

The mean number waiting is $\lambda W_q$ by Little's law. Their mean total service is $\lambda W_q s=\rho W_q$, under the independence assumptions. Hence $W_q=\mathbb E[R]+\rho W_q$, giving

$$W_q=\frac{\lambda\mathbb E[S^2]}{2(1-\rho)}=\frac{\rho s(1+c_s^2)}{2(1-\rho)},\qquad c_s^2=\frac{\operatorname{Var}(S)}{s^2}.$$

The dimensionless squared coefficient of variation $c_s^2$ measures service variability. Exponential service has $c_s^2=1$ and recovers the earlier M/M/1 mean. Deterministic service has zero variance and halves the waiting component, although it does not halve the service itself. At utilization 0.7, the running service time gives total mean 1.38543 ms for M/D/1 versus 2.13143 ms for M/M/1.

A mixture of short decode steps and long prompt-processing jobs can have a large second moment even when average service is moderate. Splitting or isolating long work can improve latency by reducing the residual service encountered by new arrivals. The improvement is a scheduling effect, not a faster transistor. Conversely, a memory change that increases service variability through retries can worsen waiting despite reducing mean access energy. This is another route by which device reliability propagates upward.

The derivation also names its failure boundaries. Correlated arrivals, dependent service times, priorities, preemption, and batch formation require different analysis. Using a general-service mean formula does not supply a general percentile formula. In particular, the M/M/1 exponential p99 expression cannot be attached to an M/G/1 mean by substitution. Keep the model family and the metric together.

10.9 Energy efficiency has a constrained optimum

In the simple budget $e_f=p(P_0/r+e_d)$ with fixed positive $P_0$, differentiating with respect to useful rate gives $de_f/dr=-pP_0/r^2\lt0$. Energy per token improves as throughput rises because baseline power is amortized. Without latency or capacity constraints, the mathematical objective pushes toward maximum throughput. The queueing constraints provide the missing engineering boundary. A nominal energy optimum at nearly full utilization can be unacceptable to users.

If dynamic power grows nonlinearly with rate, write $P_{IT}=P_0+a r^\gamma$ for an illustrative exponent $\gamma\gt1$. Then energy per token is $P_0/r+a r^{\gamma-1}$. Setting its derivative to zero gives $r_*=[P_0/(a(\gamma-1))]^{1/\gamma}$. Unlike the linear model, this can have an interior optimum because increasing rate eventually costs more dynamic energy per output. The formula is a teaching construction; fitting $a$ and $\gamma$ requires actual power and performance measurements across operating points.

Power management can change the baseline too. Turning off idle resources lowers $P_0$ but may add wakeup latency and energy. Keeping weights resident avoids reload delays but retains memory power. A design that looks inefficient at low utilization may be competitive when consolidated onto fewer active servers. This changes the service architecture and failure domain, so the comparison should report server count, utilization distribution, and latency rather than one average device-power number.

Under a fixed facility power cap $P_{\max}$, a reduction in energy per useful token can increase sustainable useful rate approximately as $r\le P_{\max}/e_f$, subject to other constraints. Total facility energy over a fixed interval may remain unchanged because the freed capacity is used for more work. Under fixed work, the same efficiency change can reduce energy. Neither interpretation is inherently superior; they answer different questions. The comparison contract must state whether work, time, power, or service quality is held fixed.

10.10 The denominator must count useful output

A token is easy to count, but not every generated token is equally useful. A serving configuration can increase raw token rate by lowering numerical quality, shortening context, accepting more errors, or emitting longer answers. For a controlled engineering comparison, hold the task distribution and quality criterion fixed. If a fraction $q_s$ of completed jobs satisfies the criterion, a simple useful-job rate is $r_u=q_s r_j$, where $r_j$ is raw completed jobs per second. Energy per useful job is $P/r_u$.

This ratio captures retries and unusable outputs only if their energy is inside the measured power boundary. If rejected generations are omitted from the denominator but their computation is also omitted from the numerator, the reported efficiency is biased. Likewise, an accelerator-only measurement excludes host, storage, network, and cooling costs. That narrow boundary can be legitimate, but it should be named and not compared directly with facility energy.

Quantization illustrates the issue. Halving weight bytes may lower traffic, but if task success falls or more sampling attempts are needed, energy per useful result can rise. The course's random-matrix precision check is deliberately a local numerical test. A deployment claim would require a workload-specific quality evaluation with consistent prompts, lengths, and acceptance criteria. Device energy and numerical correctness meet at this boundary.

Service failures also have asymmetric costs. A transient corrected memory error may increase latency; an undetected corruption can invalidate a result; an outage can trigger replay of a large computation. A single average energy term may hide those rare but expensive events. Report reliability and tail latency alongside energy, and model recovery if it is material to the intended lifetime operation count.

10.11 Communication overlap is constrained by dependencies

Suppose a step contains compute duration $t_c$ and communication duration $t_n$. If they are independent and can use distinct resources concurrently, the lower bound is $\max(t_c,t_n)$. If communication requires the final compute output, their durations add. Real training often lies between these cases: gradients become ready layer by layer, allowing some communication to overlap backward computation while a final portion remains exposed.

Represent the work as a dependency graph whose nodes are compute or transfer operations. The longest sum of durations along any dependency path is a lower bound on step time, even with unlimited independent resources. Shared links and compute units add resource constraints beyond that graph. A memory-speed improvement affects only nodes that depend on the improved resource; it helps total time only if it shortens a currently limiting path or relieves contention.

For the earlier eight-participant ring all-reduce, the 70.028 ms estimate includes a large bandwidth term and a small startup term. Reducing payload is valuable only if numerical precision and convergence remain acceptable. Reducing local memory latency leaves a nonoverlapped collective unchanged. If a faster compute kernel exposes more of the same collective, its end-to-end gain can be much smaller than its standalone gain. This is Amdahl's reasoning expressed through an actual execution schedule.

Checkpointing has a different dependency structure. Writing a checkpoint to persistent storage can be asynchronous until buffers fill or durability is required. Faster nonvolatile storage may reduce exposed pause time, improve recovery-point frequency, or lower host-memory pressure. Those are plausible roles for a new storage technology, but require its write bandwidth, endurance, consistency, and failure-recovery behavior. They cannot be inferred from a low-energy read primitive.

10.12 Measure the intervention without moving the goalposts

A useful experiment records the same workload under baseline and changed configurations. Keep model, numerical quality, context distribution, batching policy, arrival trace, and service target fixed unless the experiment explicitly studies one of them. Warm up both configurations consistently and separate initialization from steady operation. Measure elapsed time, accepted useful work, errors, and energy over matching intervals. Report enough repetitions to distinguish variation from the claimed effect.

Paired runs can reduce noise when both configurations face the same workload trace. Randomizing order can reduce drift from temperature or background activity. Power sampling must be fast enough to capture the energy of short bursts or use an integrating meter over a sufficiently long interval. A static nameplate power multiplied by execution time is an estimate, not a measured energy trace. If it is used, state the assumption and its likely direction of error.

The capstone's predicted facility saving is a tiny fraction of a millijoule per token because its assumed baseline power dominates. That difference is much smaller than plausible uncertainty in unmeasured coefficients. The numerical result is still useful: it demonstrates insensitivity under the chosen budget. It should guide measurement priorities toward baseline power, interface bandwidth, service variability, and read-margin feasibility rather than encourage reporting many digits as physical accuracy.

1. Recompute the operating point for a p99 target

Use the original utilization grid and require M/M/1 p99 response at most 5 ms. Which point is selected, and why does energy per token rise?

Solution. The analytic bound is 0.411063, so 0.3 is the largest allowed grid point. Throughput falls relative to 0.7 while the assumed 45 W baseline stays unchanged. Consequently $P_0/r$ rises. This is the price of headroom under the specified stochastic model. A less variable service process or a different scheduler can improve the tradeoff, but needs a new latency model rather than reusing the old mean constraint.

2. Diagnose a misleading energy benchmark

Configuration A emits twice as many tokens per second at the same power but only half as many outputs pass the fixed task criterion. Has useful efficiency improved?

Solution. If tokens per completed job and all other conditions are comparable, doubling raw rate and halving success fraction leaves useful rate unchanged, so energy per useful job is unchanged. If output lengths differ, token rate cannot even establish the job-rate ratio. Measure the actual accepted task outputs and include failed attempts in the energy interval. The benchmark must define useful work before interpreting throughput.

3. Explain why deterministic service still queues

Why is M/D/1 waiting nonzero even though every service takes exactly the same time?

Solution. Poisson arrivals remain variable. Several jobs can arrive close together and must wait behind one another. Eliminating service variance removes one source of waiting but not arrival randomness. At the same mean service and utilization, the mean waiting component is half the exponential-service value under these assumptions. It is not zero, and the total response still includes service time.

10.13 Decide whether a calculated saving is distinguishable

Let measured energy per useful token be $e=P/r$ over a consistent interval. For small relative perturbations, differentiating gives $\delta e/e\simeq\delta P/P-\delta r/r$. If power and rate errors are independent with relative standard uncertainties $u_P$ and $u_r$, the relative uncertainty is approximately $\sqrt{u_P^2+u_r^2}$. If they are correlated, a covariance term must be included. This is first-order uncertainty propagation, valid when errors are small and the denominator is well away from zero.

For a difference between baseline and changed configurations, shared systematic errors can partly cancel in a paired comparison, while drifting conditions can increase uncertainty. A meter calibration error common to both runs is different from a temperature difference that changes one run's actual power. Report absolute measurements and the difference with a method appropriate to the error structure. More displayed digits do not reduce either source of uncertainty.

The capstone changes facility energy from about 3.184157 to 3.183914 mJ/token in one feasible sensitivity case, a difference of roughly 0.000243 mJ/token, or 0.0076%. The coefficients feeding that calculation are assumptions rather than measurements. The appropriate conclusion is that the chosen intervention has negligible influence within this budget. It would be misleading to describe the last digits as a forecast that a physical server experiment should reproduce.

A useful sensitivity table perturbs baseline power, interface bandwidth, service variability, and memory energy separately while holding the task fixed. Then perturb them jointly under plausible correlations. If the ranking of interventions remains stable over the range, the model can support a research priority even when it cannot support a precise savings percentage. If the ranking changes, identify which measurement would settle the decision. This is a more informative outcome than reporting a single nominal optimum.

4. Compare intervention size with measurement uncertainty

A paired benchmark estimates a 0.1% energy reduction but its uncertainty in the difference is 0.5%. What can be concluded?

Solution. The experiment does not resolve a reduction of that size under the stated uncertainty. It may be consistent with a small benefit, no change, or a small regression depending on the interval construction. Improve the measurement design or report an upper bound on a practically meaningful effect. Do not turn statistical non-resolution into proof that the device has no value in a different architecture or workload.

5. A tighter mean-response target

What utilization bound follows from a 2 ms target with the same service time?

Solution. $\rho\le1-0.0006394304/0.002=0.6802848$. The original 0.7 choice becomes infeasible. Among the original grid points, 0.5 is the highest feasible point. This conclusion is about a mean under the queue model, not p99.

6. Energy target as a downward requirement

At 17,515.589 tokens/s and PUE 1.2, can a 2 mJ/token facility target be reached by eliminating all modeled dynamic energy while keeping baseline power 45 W?

Solution. No. Baseline alone contributes $1.2(45)/17515.589=3.08297$ mJ/token. The target requires lower baseline power, higher feasible throughput, a different workload/system, or a different accounting boundary. A lower-energy memory alone cannot satisfy it.

7. Amdahl's limiting case

What is the maximum speedup if only 5% of execution time can be improved?

Solution. Let $g\to\infty$: $S\to1/0.95=1.05263$. This bound assumes the unchanged fraction truly remains unchanged. Redesign can alter the fraction, but then requires a new model and evidence.

8. Training communication

In the eight-participant example, halve payload to 0.5 GB. Is all-reduce time exactly halved?

Solution. The bandwidth term falls from 70 to 35 ms; the 28 µs latency term remains. New time is 35.028 ms, slightly above half of 70.028 ms. Reducing precision might reduce payload, but gradient accuracy and convergence must remain acceptable.
